You are writing the script for a 20-25 second YouTube Short about ONE

the twist. One idea, told tight enough that viewers watch to the very
end -- not a list of several facts.

Avoid these specific facts too (already used, even if under a different

Requirements:
- Pick ONE specific, narrow fact -- a real number, name, event, animal,
  object or place -- and give it three beats:
  - Beat 1 (~15-20 words): the payoff, stated concretely (see the
    first-line rule below).
  - Beat 2 (~20-25 words): the real why/how, with one concrete detail (a
    date, a name, a number, a place) that makes it feel real.
  - Beat 3 (~15-20 words): the twist or reaction -- a second layer the
    viewer didn't see coming, or a genuine opinion about it. It must
    still be about THIS fact; no generic wrap-up that would fit any
    video.
- Every beat must add NEW information. Never restate beat 1's claim in
  a later beat, and never reuse the same phrase or sentence shape twice
  ("it actually works" ... "because it actually works"; "that's how
  they X" ... "it's how they X"). In a ~22s script any repeat is
  obvious, and the review step rejects it -- real 2026-09-26 run: 3 of
  6 videos were dropped for exactly this. Each beat should also open
  with different words than the others.
- At least one beat MUST carry a genuine opinion or reaction that goes
  beyond restating the fact -- non-negotiable, same as the rest of the
  channel.
- Total SCRIPT length across all three beats: 50-65 words -- a HARD
  limit, spoken in about 20-25 seconds (edge_tts paces ~2.6 words/sec).
  Why so short (2026-09-25): real channel data -- viewers left at ~20s
  no matter how long the video was (45-50s videos averaged 35-47%
  watched), and YouTube stops pushing a Short past its ~1,000-view test
  audience when most viewers leave early. A 22s video watched for 20s
  is nearly complete, and replays count.
  Conversational pace, no filler, no "did you know" openers, no
  numbering out loud, no "subscribe"/"follow" lines.
- The very first line must lead with the PAYOFF, not build up to it —
  the single most concrete, surprising, specific detail of the fact (a
  real number, name, or outcome), stated directly. Two ways this still
  goes wrong even beyond the "no scene-setting" rule:
  1. A flat description that gives the whole fact away with nothing left
     to explain — still banned, same as before.
  2. A generic tease that could be pasted onto almost any fact ("nobody
     knows why", "scientists are still stumped", "you won't believe
     what happens next") — this spends the first couple seconds asking
     the viewer to trust you instead of giving them something real. If
     your first line would still basically work on a completely
     different fact, it's too generic — rewrite it around the specific
     number/name/detail that makes THIS fact the one worth watching.
  The first line should be the actual payoff — the surprising claim
  itself, concretely stated. What keeps the viewer watching past it is
  wanting to know WHY or HOW, which the rest of the beat then explains.
    - BAD (flat description, answer already given, nothing left to
      explain): "Oxford has an electric bell that's been ringing since
      1840."
    - BAD (generic tease, no concrete payoff — could be about anything):
      "This bell has a secret that scientists still can't figure out."
    - GOOD (payoff-first — the actual concrete claim, stated plainly):
      "This bell has rung over 10 billion times on the same two
      batteries, and no one's allowed to open it to find out why."
    - BAD (flat description): "The guillotine is named after a doctor
      who opposed the death penalty."
    - GOOD (payoff-first — the specific twist, stated as fact): "The
      doctor the guillotine is named after spent his final years
      begging France to take his name off it."
- For each beat, act as the visual director, not just a keyword
  extractor. The viewer wants to see the actual interesting thing being
  described, not generic footage that merely relates to the topic. For
  each beat, give:
  - SUBJECT: the exact primary subject in 2-5 words — the specific
    object, animal, place, invention, food, or thing the fact is
    actually about (e.g. "Oxford Electric Bell", not "science" or
    "electricity").
  - EXACT_QUERIES: 2-3 comma-separated search queries most likely to
    find footage of the SUBJECT itself, or as close to it as a stock
    library could realistically have (e.g. "Oxford electric bell",
    "vintage electric bell apparatus").
  - REPRESENTATION_QUERIES: 2-3 comma-separated queries for an
    accurate stand-in if the exact subject isn't findable — the same
    TYPE of thing, not a broader category (e.g. "antique brass bell
    mechanism", "Victorian scientific instrument").
  - CONCEPT_QUERIES: 0-2 comma-separated queries for a visual
    explanation of the mechanism/process/scale involved, if the fact is
    about HOW something works rather than a physical object (e.g.
    "electric circuit demonstration"). Leave empty (just write "none")
    if the fact doesn't need this.
  - If the fact's core idea is a number, a word, a statistic, or another
    non-visual abstraction (like "the word 'set' has 400+ meanings" or "a
    speeding fine cost $103,000"), the SUBJECT and queries should target
    the concrete real-world thing the fact is actually ABOUT (a
    dictionary, a courtroom, the specific animal/place/object involved),
    never the abstraction itself.
  - Never use broad generic queries as EXACT_QUERIES or
    REPRESENTATION_QUERIES: "science", "technology", "nature", "history",
    "interesting", "surprising", "counting", "money" are all too vague to
    show the actual thing — if a query like that is genuinely the best
    available, it belongs in CONCEPT_QUERIES or is a sign the SUBJECT
    needs to be more specific instead.
  - GOOD example (Oxford Electric Bell fact): SUBJECT "Oxford Electric
    Bell"; EXACT_QUERIES "Oxford electric bell, historic electric bell
    apparatus"; REPRESENTATION_QUERIES "antique brass bell mechanism,
    Victorian scientific instrument"; CONCEPT_QUERIES "electromagnetic
    bell demonstration".
